# DDPM vs DDIM

I've started to learn more about diffusion models. I got bit confused by DDPM and DDIM. I will lay down my own thoughts here for my future reference.

## It all started from Bayes

$$
\begin{align}
q(x_{t-1} \mid x_{t}, x_{0}) = \frac{q(x_{t} \mid x_{t-1}, x_{0})q(x_{t-1} \mid x_{0})}{q(x_{t} \mid x_{0})} 
\end{align}
$$

Where $x_{0}$ is the initial image and $x_{t}$ is the noise-added image at time step $t$.

We assume $q(x_{t}\mid x_{0})$ to be Gaussian. The key question is how to handle $q(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1},x_{0})$.

## DDPM

DDPM starts by assuming the forward process is Markov. This means

$$
\begin{align}
q(x_{t} \mid x_{t-1}, x_{0}) = q(x_{t} \mid x_{t-1})
\end{align}
$$

In the original paper, it assumes

$$
\begin{align}
x_{t} = \alpha_{t}x_{t-1} + \beta_{t}\epsilon_{t}
\end{align}
$$

where $\alpha_{t}$ and $\beta_{t}$ are coefficients satisfying $\alpha_t^2+\beta_t^2=1$, and $\epsilon_{t} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.

We can also write

$$
\begin{align}
x_{t} = \tilde{\alpha}_{t}x_{0} + \tilde{\beta}_{t}\tilde{\epsilon}_{t}
\end{align}
$$

where $\tilde{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\alpha_{i}$ and $\tilde{\beta}_{t}=\sqrt{1-\tilde{\alpha}_{t}^{2}}$.

Now we can plug everything into Bayes to get the forward process posterior:

$$
\begin{align}
q(x_{t-1} \mid x_{t}, x_{0}) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}\tilde{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}}{\tilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}}x_{t}+\frac{\tilde{\alpha}_{t-1}\beta_{t}^{2}}{\tilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}}x_{0},\ \frac{\tilde{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}\beta_{t}^{2}}{\tilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}}I\right)
\end{align}
$$

Importantly, the variance is fixed here — it depends only on the noise schedule, not on the data.

## DDIM

In DDIM, we do not directly model the forward process $q(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1})$. Instead, we directly model $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t},x_{0})$ as Gaussian:

$$
\begin{align}
x_{t-1} = \kappa_{t} x_{t} + \lambda_{t} x_{0} + \sigma_{t}\epsilon_{t}
\end{align}
$$

where $\epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. We know $x_{t}=\tilde{\alpha}_{t}x_{0}+\tilde{\beta}_{t}\tilde{\epsilon}_{t}$, so

$$
\begin{align}
x_{t-1} &=& \kappa_{t} (\tilde{\alpha}_{t}x_{0} + \tilde{\beta}_{t}\tilde{\epsilon}) + \lambda_{t} x_{0} + \sigma_{t}\epsilon_{t} \\
&=& ( \kappa_{t}\tilde{\alpha}_{t} + \lambda_{t})x_{0} + \kappa_{t}\tilde{\beta}_{t}\tilde{\epsilon}_{t} + \sigma_{t}\epsilon_{t}
\end{align}
$$
> $\epsilon_{t}$ and $\tilde{\epsilon}_{t}$ are independent.

By comparing to

$$
\begin{align}
x_{t-1} = \tilde{\alpha}_{t-1}x_{0} + \tilde{\beta}_{t-1}\tilde{\epsilon}_{t-1}
\end{align}
$$

and matching the mean and variance of $x_{t-1}$, we get two constraints:

$$
\begin{align}
\tilde{\alpha}_{t-1} &=& \kappa_{t}\tilde{\alpha}_{t} + \lambda_{t} \\
\tilde{\beta}_{t-1}^{2} &=& \kappa_{t}^{2}\tilde{\beta}_{t}^{2} + \sigma_{t}^{2}
\end{align}
$$

Solving these equations gives:

$$
\begin{align}
\kappa_{t} &=& \frac{\sqrt{\tilde{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}-\sigma_{t}^{2}}}{\tilde{\beta}_{t}} \\
\lambda_{t} &=& \tilde{\alpha}_{t-1} - \tilde{\alpha}_{t}\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}-\sigma_{t}^{2}}}{\tilde{\beta}_{t}}
\end{align}
$$

Note both $\kappa_{t}$ and $\lambda_{t}$ depend on $\sigma_{t}$, which we can choose freely. This gives us:

$$
\begin{align}
q(x_{t-1} \mid x_{t}, x_{0}) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}-\sigma_{t}^{2}}}{\tilde{\beta}_{t}}x_{t} + \left(\tilde{\alpha}_{t-1} - \tilde{\alpha}_{t}\frac{\sqrt{\tilde{\beta}_{t-1}^{2}-\sigma_{t}^{2}}}{\tilde{\beta}_{t}}\right)x_{0},\ \sigma_{t}^{2}I\right)
\end{align}
$$

If we choose $\sigma_t=\frac{\tilde{\beta}_{t-1}\beta_t}{\tilde{\beta}_t}$, this recovers exactly the DDPM posterior. This means DDPM is a special case of DDIM with a specific choice of $\sigma_t$.

Since we can choose $\sigma_{t}$ freely, we can also set $\sigma_{t}=0$. In this case the reverse process becomes **deterministic** — given the same $x_T$, the same $x_0$ is always produced. The forward process is also no longer required to be Markov.

## What's next?

At this point, the derivations of DDPM and DDIM converge.

We actually care about $p_{\theta}(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t})$ rather than $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t},x_{0})$, because $x_{0}$ is what we want to generate — if we already had it, there would be nothing to do.

The solution is to approximate $x_{0}$ from $x_{t}$ using a neural network parametermized by $\theta$. There are two equivalent parameterizations:

**$x_0$-prediction**: train $f_{\theta}(x_{t}, t)$ directly to predict the clean image:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L} = \|x_{0} - f_{\theta}(x_{t}, t)\|^{2}
\end{align}
$$

**$\epsilon$-prediction** (used in the original DDPM paper): train $\epsilon_{\theta}(x_{t}, t)$ to predict the noise, then recover $x_0$ via:
$$
\begin{align}
\hat{x}_0 = \frac{x_t - \tilde{\beta}_t\, \epsilon_{\theta}(x_t, t)}{\tilde{\alpha}_t}
\end{align}
$$

The two are mathematically equivalent but differ empirically — noise prediction tends to produce better sample quality in practice, which is why it is the standard choice.

This means we can reuse models trained in DDPM in DDIM. The only difference is sampling.

## Wrong Thought Process About DDIM
I had a wrong thought process which confused me a bit. Here it is. If we know the marginals

$$
\begin{align}
x_{t} &=& \tilde{\alpha}_{t}x_{0} + \tilde{\beta}_{t}\tilde{\epsilon}_{t} \\
x_{t-1} &=& \tilde{\alpha}_{t-1}x_{0} + \tilde{\beta}_{t-1}\tilde{\epsilon}_{t-1}
\end{align}
$$
why cannot we derive $x_{t}$ from $x_{t-1}$ with random noise $\tilde{\epsilon}_{t}$ and $\tilde{\epsilon}_{t-1}$ and claim Markov property? The issue here is $\tilde{\epsilon}_{t}$ and $\tilde{\epsilon}_{t-1}$ are not necessarily independent. They might be correlated due to we only modeling marginal distributions. As a result we cannot claim Markov property.

## Summary

* DDPM: defines the forward process as Markov chain: $q(x_{t}\mid x_{t=1})=\mathcal{N}\left(\alpha_{t}x_{t-1},\beta_{t}^{2}I\right)$. This choise fully determines everything
   * $q(x_{t}\mid x_{0})$: fixed by the Markov chain
   * $q(x_{t-1}\mid x_{t},x_{0})$: fixed by Bayes, no free parameters

* DDIM: starts from the marginals $q(x_{t}\mid x_{0})=\mathcal{N}\left(\tilde{{\alpha}}_{t}x_{t-1},\tilde{\beta}_{t}^{2}I\right)$. Then it tries find joint distributions $q(x_{t-1},x_{t}\mid x_{0})$ which are consistent with the marginals.
